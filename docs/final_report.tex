\section{Introduction}

Face-recognition systems perform well on clear, well-lit, frontal faces similar to their training images, but degrade sharply when identity-bearing regions are hidden by a mask, sunglasses, a scarf, a cap, or lighting/pose changes. This project addresses \textbf{closed-set face identification}: every test image belongs to an already-enrolled person, and the system returns the matching participant ID (e.g.\ \texttt{p07}) rather than just detecting an occlusion's presence.

The system uses a consent-based dataset of 30 participants captured under eight covering conditions and two lighting levels, processed through a fixed detect-align-crop pipeline, and classified with a \textbf{pretrained ArcFace (iResNet50) embedding} paired with a \textbf{RetinaFace} detector. Three experiments test how much task-specific training helps, and whether occlusion-diverse training data is necessary under compound occlusions. The system was packaged into a live phone-camera demonstration served from a laptop.

This report documents what was collected and built, and compares the approach against closely related published work.

\section{Problem Statement and Objectives}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given an image of a normal or partially occluded face, the system must classify it as one of 30 enrolled identities. Different occlusions hide different regions: a mask removes the nose/mouth/jawline, sunglasses the eyes, a cap the forehead/hairline, low light reduces texture generally. The central question: can a pretrained face-embedding model, given only a few hundred images per identity, stay discriminative under these conditions without being retrained on masks or sunglasses from scratch?

\subsection{Objectives and Completion Status}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item Collect a structured face-image dataset covering a range of occlusion and lighting conditions. \textbf{Completed} — 30 participants, 6{,}000 raw images.
\item Capture each participant under eight covering conditions: none, cap, mask, sunglasses, scarf, clear glasses, mask+sunglasses, mask+clear glasses. \textbf{Completed.}
\item Detect, align, crop, resize, and quality-check every face image using one reproducible preprocessing pipeline. \textbf{Completed} — 5{,}988/6{,}000 images (99.8\%) successfully detected and aligned.
\item Split the dataset into train/validation/test sets such that every identity, and where possible every identity--occlusion combination, appears in all three splits. \textbf{Completed.}
\item Evaluate a pretrained ArcFace pipeline with two forms of task-specific training: a trained classification head, and partial backbone fine-tuning. \textbf{Completed} — three experiments, E1--E3.
\item Evaluate overall accuracy, per-participant accuracy, and per-occlusion accuracy. \textbf{Completed} — extended beyond the original plan to also include per-occlusion precision/recall/F1 and a full $30\times30$ confusion matrix for every experiment (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item Produce a working demonstration that predicts the enrolled participant from a live phone camera. \textbf{Completed} — a browser-based demo streamed from a phone to a laptop over HTTPS on the local network.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Team and Participants}

Thirty identities (\texttt{p01}--\texttt{p30}) were enrolled: six team members each recruited and photographed five people (themselves plus four consenting relatives/acquaintances). The participant-to-photographer mapping lives in \texttt{metadata/person\_id\_mapping.txt}, kept internal rather than reproduced here.

\subsection{Consent}

Every participant signed a consent form before any photograph. It explains this is an academic, non-commercial prototype; each participant gets an anonymous ID (e.g.\ \texttt{p07}), never a name; raw images and the identity mapping stay restricted to the six members; participation is voluntary and withdrawable; and a recognisable photo appears in the report/demo only for participants who separately tick an optional permission box.

\subsection{Capture Protocol}

Each participant was photographed under eight covering conditions --- \texttt{none}, \texttt{cap}, \texttt{mask}, \texttt{scarf}, \texttt{clearglass}, \texttt{sunglass}, \texttt{mask\_clearglass}, \texttt{mask\_sunglass} --- crossed with two lighting levels, \texttt{regular} and \texttt{low}, in a single sitting, with head position varying naturally across shots rather than a logged pose label. The dataset totals \textbf{6{,}000 images}: 30 identities $\times$ 200 images, well beyond the proposal's original 10--15-participant target.

Filenames follow a fixed pattern (e.g.\ \texttt{p07\_mask\_sunglass\_low\_014.jpg}), so identity, occlusion, and lighting parse without a lookup table; a manifest CSV is the authoritative metadata source.

\subsection{A Note on Synthetic Images}
\label{sec:synthetic}

The large majority of the dataset is real photographs: every identity was photographed wearing every occlusion in person. A small minority are an exception: a few participants declined to physically wear a specific occlusion (most often sunglasses, for personal or religious reasons), so that occlusion was digitally added to one of their clean photos instead, just so that identity's coverage would not be entirely missing. These are a small, unflagged fraction of the dataset -- disclosed here, not claimed to be common.

Separately, the training-only augmentation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:model}) applies mild synthetic transformations --- flips, rotations, brightness/contrast jitter, resolution degradation, blur, sensor noise, JPEG re-compression, random erasing --- to simulate the live phone-camera domain. These are training-time variations of real photos, not new identities or occlusions.

\section{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Every accepted photo passes through one fixed pipeline (\texttt{scripts/01\_preprocess.py}), identical for training, validation, test, and later, the live demo. Figure~\ref{fig:preprocess-pipeline} shows the flow.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.6cm]
\node[pbox] (raw) {Raw photo\\(full resolution)};
\node[pbox, right=of raw] (det) {RetinaFace detect\\bbox + 5 landmarks};
\node[pbox, right=of det] (align) {Similarity transform\\align to template};
\node[pboxend, right=of align] (crop) {$112\times112$ crop\\(training-ready)};
\draw[parrow] (raw) -- (det);
\draw[parrow] (det) -- (align);
\draw[parrow] (align) -- (crop);
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Preprocessing pipeline: every accepted photo, before it can be used for training, evaluation, or live inference.}
\label{fig:preprocess-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Detect.} RetinaFace (\texttt{insightface}'s \texttt{buffalo\_l} detector, \texttt{det\_10g.onnx}) locates the face and its five landmarks at \texttt{det\_size=(640,640)}, \texttt{det\_thresh=0.5}. The largest detected face is kept.
\item \textbf{Fallback on failure.} If nothing is detected, the image is retried at \texttt{det\_thresh=0.3}, then at 2$\times$ upscale. Every failure is logged, never dropped silently.
\item \textbf{Align and crop.} A similarity transform (\texttt{insightface.utils.face\_align.norm\_crop}) maps the five landmarks onto ArcFace's reference template, producing a $112\times112$ aligned crop -- this is what stops the classifier keying on background or camera artifacts.
\item \textbf{Log.} Each image's path, identity, occlusion, lighting, MD5 hash, and detection outcome are appended to \texttt{metadata/preprocess\_log.csv}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Result:} 5{,}988 of 6{,}000 images (99.8\%) were successfully detected and aligned, above the 97\% acceptance gate. The 12 failures concentrated in the hardest conditions --- 10 in \texttt{mask\_sunglass}, 2 in \texttt{mask} --- where too little face remained visible for landmark detection even at the loosened threshold.

\section{Data Organisation and Split}
\label{sec:split}

\subsection{Group-Aware Splitting}

Splitting is not simple random assignment: near-duplicate images (a burst of photos seconds apart, or a low-light image derived by darkening a regular one) could otherwise land on opposite sides of the split and inflate test accuracy. (Exact, byte-identical duplicates from repeat shutter presses also exist and are treated as independent images; every MD5 hash is logged for auditability.) Two images are grouped, and forced into the same split, if:
\begin{itemize}
\item they are derived from the same source image, or
\item they share a person \emph{and} occlusion and were captured within a 10-second window, with burst-chains capped at 8 seconds to prevent one long burst from swallowing an entire condition.
\end{itemize}
Groups are then assigned as whole units via a deficit-based greedy allocator: largest groups first, each into whichever split has the biggest shortfall against its 60/20/20 target, per (person, occlusion) cell. This avoids the naive sequential-fill failure seen during development, which produced a wildly unbalanced split (train 24\%, val 58\%, test 18\%) from a single 49-image group.

\subsection{Coverage Guarantee}

A hard requirement was enforced: \textbf{every participant must appear in all three splits.} A repair pass can force-move whole groups from train into an under-represented split if needed; it was not triggered in the final run. Per-occlusion coverage per split is best-effort only: 36 of 240 (person $\times$ occlusion) cells are absent from test specifically (informational, not a failure).

\section{Model and Training Approach}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Selected Models}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RetinaFace} \cite{deng2020retinaface} (\texttt{det\_10g.onnx}) — detection and five-point landmark alignment (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}).
\item \textbf{Pretrained ArcFace} \cite{deng2019arcface}, iResNet50 backbone (\texttt{w600k\_r50.onnx}, \texttt{insightface} \texttt{buffalo\_l}) — turns each aligned $112\times112$ crop into a 512-dimensional embedding. This is the primary identification model in every experiment below.
\end{itemize}

RetinaFace and ArcFace were selected because they are purpose-built for faces, need far less local data than training from scratch, run on both the remote GPU box and a laptop CPU, and have precedent adapting the same family to occluded faces (Section~\ref{sec:related}). No from-scratch CNN baseline was trained: with only 30 classes and a few hundred images per identity, it was judged inherently weaker than adapting a pretrained backbone -- effort went to the fine-tuning ladder below instead.

\subsection{Experiments}

Three experiments were run, each answering a specific question about how much task-specific training the pretrained embedding actually needs:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Experiment design.}
\begin{tabular}{L{1cm}L{3cm}L{3.6cm}L{6cm}}

\textbf{ID} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Backbone} & \textbf{Question} \\
\midrule

\textbf{E3} goes further, unfreezing the last 15\% of the backbone's own parameters alongside a new identity head, letting the network slightly adjust its face representation instead of only fitting a boundary on a fixed one. Only the last layers are unfrozen: with just a few thousand training images, adjusting the whole backbone risks overwriting its general face knowledge, and the final layers encode the most identity-specific detail anyway. Batch-normalisation statistics stay frozen throughout, since updating them on so little data was found to degrade the pretrained features. Since \texttt{insightface} ships ArcFace as inference-only, the backbone was converted to a trainable form and verified to match the original (outputs agreed to four decimal places) before training. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-e3} shows the pipeline.

\textbf{How the validation split was used.} It never contributes to any reported accuracy, but does two jobs: deciding when to stop training (once validation performance stops improving, so the model isn't over-memorising train data), and guiding setting choices during development --- margin strength, learning rates, how much backbone to unfreeze --- by comparing candidates on validation before ever touching test data.

No augmentation was applied to validation or test data at any stage; the live-capture-style augmentations described in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} were applied only to E1/E2/E3 training images.

\textbf{E1 vs.\ E2 is the clearest, most informative comparison here.} E1 (clean-only) drops to 75.5\% precision / 71.4\% recall / 68.9\% F1 specifically on \texttt{mask+sunglasses} — the one pair hiding both the eyes and the lower face — while scoring a perfect 100.0\% on every single-occlusion condition. E2 (all eight conditions) recovers to a perfect 100.0\% on that same condition and 99.90\% top-1 overall. This is direct evidence that occlusion-diverse training data matters specifically for compound occlusions, not single ones, which a pretrained embedding already handles well unaided.

\textbf{E3 reached 100.00\% top-1}, correctly classifying every test image. Since E2 was already at 99.90\%, E3's gain is small -- it closes the last gap rather than solving one E2 left substantially open. With both at or above 99.9\%, the more informative result here is the E1-vs-E2 comparison above, not the marginal E2-vs-E3 gap.

\section{Deployment — Live Demonstration}
\label{sec:deployment}

\subsection{Model Selection and Live Switching}

A dropdown selects between E1, E2, and E3 live, without restarting the server. \textbf{E2 is the default}, since it is close to E3's accuracy (99.90\% vs.\ 100.00\%) without needing E3's heavier backbone loaded first. A per-identity \emph{gallery} classifier with a calibrated \texttt{UNKNOWN}-rejection threshold (0.3179) is implemented (\texttt{scripts/07\_build\_gallery.py}) but currently disabled in the dropdown; consequently E1/E2/E3 are all closed-set only -- every detected face is assigned one of the 30 identities, with no mechanism yet active to flag an unenrolled visitor.

\subsection{Live Phone-Camera Demo}

The demo runs as a Flask server on a laptop, serving a browser page to a phone over the local Wi-Fi network via HTTPS. The phone's camera streams frames roughly every 400\,ms (900\,ms for E3); each frame is detected, aligned, embedded by whichever model is selected, averaged over a rolling buffer (cleared on mode switch, since E3 uses a different embedding space), and classified. The result renders on the phone screen in real time. A camera-lens picker and front/back toggle are included, since many phones default to a blurry ultra-wide rear lens. Figure~\ref{fig:demo-ui} shows the idle state; Figure~\ref{fig:inference-pipeline} shows the full pipeline.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Closed set only.} Every input is forced into one of 30 identities (E1/E2/E3, none reject an unenrolled face); a gallery-based classifier with a calibrated rejection threshold exists but is disabled (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}). No formal open-set evaluation was performed.
\item \textbf{30 identities.} Results will not extrapolate to hundreds or thousands of enrolled people, and are not comparable in absolute terms to the open-set results of Section~\ref{sec:comparison}.
\item \textbf{No anti-spoofing or liveness detection.} A laboratory prototype, not an access-control system.
\item \textbf{Future work} suggested by Section~\ref{sec:related}'s base papers: an auxiliary mask-detection objective alongside identity \cite{montero2022mtarcface}, or a larger identity count to test whether \cite{chong2022svm}'s SVM-style scaling limits have any analogue here -- both testable on a second, multi-session capture round.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This project built and evaluated a closed-set face-identification system robust to real occlusions, using a pretrained ArcFace model. Thirty participants were recruited, and 6{,}000 images collected across eight occlusion conditions and two lighting levels — well beyond Pgu-Face's scale. A reproducible pipeline processed 99.8\% of the dataset, and a duplicate-aware, coverage-guaranteed split produced a 3{,}546/1{,}444/998 partition. Three experiments showed that a head trained only on clean faces degrades specifically on \texttt{mask+sunglasses} (E1, 94.29\% overall, 71.4\% on that condition), training on occlusion-diverse data recovers it (E2, 99.90\%), and partial backbone fine-tuning closes the remaining gap entirely (E3, 100.00\%). The system was deployed as a live phone-camera demonstration, defaulting to E2 with E1/E3 selectable via dropdown. Against its two base papers, this project is not offered as beating either — Pgu-Face proposes no recognition model, and Chong et al.'s result comes from a different dataset and protocol — but as a practical adaptation: a pretrained representation over a from-scratch CNN+SVM pipeline, a broader occlusion set, and a working live deployment neither attempts. The honest contribution is the controlled E1-vs-E2 result: occlusion-diverse training data, not architecture or loss engineering, closed the gap on the hardest occlusion condition.
